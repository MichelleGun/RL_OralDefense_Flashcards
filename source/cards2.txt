@card w6
Q: What are Monte Carlo methods in RL and what are their requirements?
A: **Monte Carlo** methods estimate values by **averaging complete sampled returns** from experience. By the law of large numbers the average converges to the expected return.
Requirements and properties:
- **Model-free**: needs only experience or a simulator, no $P$ or $R$.
- **Episodic tasks only**: you must wait until the episode ends to know $G_t$.
- **No bootstrapping**: targets are real returns, so estimates are unbiased but high variance.
- Updates happen episode by episode, not step by step.
- Can focus on just the states you care about.
K: Monte Carlo, sample average, model-free, episodic, no bootstrapping

@card w6
Q: First-visit vs every-visit MC. Do the numeric example.
A: Episode A, B, A, C, terminal with rewards 1, 2, 1, 5 and $\gamma=1$. Returns: from first A $G=9$, from B $G=8$, from second A $G=6$, from C $G=5$.
- **First-visit**: only the first visit to each state counts. $V(A)=9$, $V(B)=8$, $V(C)=5$.
- **Every-visit**: every visit counts. $V(A) = (9+6)/2 = 7.5$, $V(B)=8$, $V(C)=5$.
First-visit samples are independent across episodes and unbiased. Every-visit is biased for finite samples (returns within an episode are correlated) but consistent, simpler to code and extends naturally to function approximation. Both converge to $v_\pi$.
K: first-visit MC, every-visit MC, returns, bias

@card w6
Q: Why does Monte Carlo control estimate Q instead of V?
A: Without a model, $V$ is not enough to act. Greedy improvement from $V$ needs $\pi'(s)=\arg\max_a [R(s,a) + \gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)V(s')]$, which uses $P$ and $R$.
With $Q$, improvement is just $\pi'(s) = \arg\max_a Q(s,a)$, which is model-free. So every model-free control method (MC control, SARSA, Q-learning, DQN) learns action values.
K: action values, model-free control, greedy improvement

@card w6
Q: What is the exploration problem in MC control, and what are exploring starts and soft policies?
A: If the policy is deterministic you only ever see one action per state, so you cannot compare actions.
- **Exploring starts**: every episode begins at a random state-action pair with non-zero probability for each. Guarantees coverage, but unrealistic with real systems (you cannot start a real robot in any state you like).
- **Soft policies**: keep $\pi(a\mid s)>0$ for all actions, for example $\varepsilon$-greedy. On-policy MC control with $\varepsilon$-soft policies converges to the best $\varepsilon$-soft policy, not the truly optimal one, unless $\varepsilon$ decays.
K: exploring starts, soft policy, epsilon-soft, coverage

@card w6
Q: What is GLIE and why do we need it?
A: **Greedy in the Limit with Infinite Exploration**:
1. Every state-action pair is visited infinitely often.
2. The policy converges to greedy.
A fixed $\varepsilon$ never becomes fully greedy, so it can only reach the best $\varepsilon$-soft policy. Decaying, for example $\varepsilon_k = 1/k$, satisfies both conditions. GLIE MC control converges to $q_*$.
K: GLIE, decaying epsilon, convergence

@card w6
Q: On-policy vs off-policy. Explain with target and behaviour policies.
A: **Target policy** $\pi$: the policy you want to learn about. **Behaviour policy** $b$: the policy that actually generates the data.
- **On-policy**: $\pi = b$. You learn about the exploratory policy you are running (SARSA, MC control with $\varepsilon$-greedy).
- **Off-policy**: $\pi \neq b$. You learn about, say, the greedy policy while acting exploratorily, or learn from someone else's data (Q-learning, off-policy MC with importance sampling, DQN).
Off-policy needs **coverage**: $\pi(a\mid s) > 0 \Rightarrow b(a\mid s) > 0$.
Correct a line in your notes: the learner updates the **target** policy from the behaviour data. In Q-learning both are derived from the same Q table, so the behaviour policy changes too, but that is a side effect, not the definition.
K: on-policy, off-policy, target policy, behaviour policy, coverage

@card w6
Q: What is importance sampling and why is it needed for off-policy MC?
A: Returns collected under $b$ have expectation $v_b$, not $v_\pi$. Re-weight each return by how much more likely the trajectory is under $\pi$:
$$\rho_{t:T-1} = \prod_{k=t}^{T-1}\frac{\pi(A_k\mid S_k)}{b(A_k\mid S_k)}$$
The environment's transition probabilities cancel, so the ratio needs no model.
- **Ordinary IS**: $V(s) = \frac{\sum \rho G}{N}$. Unbiased, variance can be infinite.
- **Weighted IS**: $V(s) = \frac{\sum \rho G}{\sum \rho}$. Biased (goes to 0), far lower variance, preferred.
With a greedy target, $\rho$ becomes 0 as soon as $b$ takes a non-greedy action, so off-policy MC control only learns from the tails of episodes.
K: importance sampling, ratio, ordinary IS, weighted IS, variance

@card w6
Q: Compare Monte Carlo and Dynamic Programming.
A: Side by side:
- Model needed: DP yes, MC no.
- Bootstraps: DP yes, MC no.
- Needs episodes to terminate: DP no, MC yes.
- Backup: DP full (all successors, weighted by probability), MC sample (one real trajectory to the end).
- Updates: DP sweeps over all states, MC only the states it visits.
- MC is less hurt when the Markov property is violated, because it does not bootstrap.
K: MC vs DP, bootstrapping, full backup, sample backup

@card w7
Q: What is TD learning? Write TD(0) and name its parts.
A: **TD learning** is model-free like MC but **bootstraps** like DP: it updates after every step using its current estimate of the next state.
$$V(S_t) \leftarrow V(S_t) + \alpha\,[\,\underbrace{R_{t+1} + \gamma V(S_{t+1})}_{\text{TD target}} - V(S_t)\,]$$
- **TD target**: $R_{t+1} + \gamma V(S_{t+1})$.
- **TD error** $\delta_t$: target minus current estimate.
- $\alpha$: step size.
It updates a guess towards a guess. Works online, in continuing tasks and with incomplete episodes.
K: TD learning, TD(0), TD target, TD error, bootstrapping

@card w7
Q: Explain the bias variance trade-off between MC and TD.
A: - MC target $G_t$ is an **unbiased** sample of $v_\pi(S_t)$ but has **high variance**, because it sums many random actions, transitions and rewards.
- TD target $R_{t+1}+\gamma V(S_{t+1})$ is **biased** because $V(S_{t+1})$ is only an estimate, but has **low variance** because it depends on one random step.
Consequences: TD usually learns faster in Markov environments and is more sensitive to initial values. MC is more robust when the Markov property fails. n-step and $\lambda$-returns sit in between.
K: bias, variance, MC, TD, n-step

@card w7
Q: Explain the driving home example.
A: You predict travel time while driving: leave office (30 min predicted), reach car in rain (40), exit highway (35), stuck behind a truck (40), home street (43), arrive (43).
- **MC** waits until you arrive, then pulls every earlier prediction towards the final 43.
- **TD** adjusts each prediction immediately towards the next prediction. When it starts raining you revise at once instead of waiting.
It shows why TD can learn during an episode and needs no final outcome.
K: driving home, MC vs TD, online learning

@card w7
Q: The AB example: why do MC and TD give different answers from the same data?
A: Eight episodes: A,0,B,0 once, B,1 six times, B,0 once. Everyone agrees $V(B) = 6/8 = 0.75$.
- **MC**: $V(A) = 0$, because the only episode through A returned 0. It minimises error on the observed returns.
- **TD**: $V(A) = 0 + V(B) = 0.75$. It assumes Markov structure: A always went to B, and B is worth 0.75.
TD's answer is the **certainty-equivalence** estimate, the value of the maximum-likelihood model built from the data. That usually generalises better, which is why batch TD tends to beat batch MC on Markov problems.
K: AB example, certainty equivalence, batch TD, maximum likelihood

@card w7
Q: Write the SARSA update. Why is it on-policy? Where does the name come from?
A: $$Q(S_t,A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t,A_t) + \alpha[R_{t+1} + \gamma Q(S_{t+1},A_{t+1}) - Q(S_t,A_t)]$$
The name is the quintuple $(S_t, A_t, R_{t+1}, S_{t+1}, A_{t+1})$.
It is **on-policy** because $A_{t+1}$ is the action the agent actually takes next with its $\varepsilon$-greedy policy, and that same action is then executed. It evaluates and improves the policy it follows, exploration included.
Numbers: $Q=10$, $R=2$, $\gamma=0.9$, $\alpha=0.5$, $Q(S',A')=8$. Target $2+7.2 = 9.2$, error $-0.8$, new $Q=9.6$.
K: SARSA, on-policy, quintuple, TD control

@card w7
Q: Write the Q-learning update. Why is it off-policy?
A: $$Q(S_t,A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t,A_t) + \alpha[R_{t+1} + \gamma \max_a Q(S_{t+1},a) - Q(S_t,A_t)]$$
It bootstraps from the **best** next action, not the one actually taken. So it learns $q_*$ (the value of the greedy target policy) whatever exploratory behaviour policy generated the data, as long as all pairs keep being visited.
Same numbers as SARSA but next-state values 8, 11, 5: target $2 + 0.9 \times 11 = 11.9$, new $Q = 10 + 0.5(1.9) = 10.95$. One substitution, max instead of the taken action, is the entire difference.
K: Q-learning, off-policy, max operator, q-star

@card w7
Q: Oral defense favourite: where is the hidden policy in Q-learning?
A: There are two policies and neither is stored as a table:
- **Behaviour policy**: $\varepsilon$-greedy with respect to $Q$. This is what actually moves the agent and collects data.
- **Target policy**: greedy with respect to $Q$. It is hidden inside the $\max_a Q(S_{t+1},a)$ term of the update, because the max is exactly the value of acting greedily from $S_{t+1}$.
The final learned policy is implicit too: $\pi(s) = \arg\max_a Q(s,a)$. Policies in Q-learning are always read off the Q table on demand.
K: Q-learning, hidden policy, target policy, behaviour policy, greedy

@card w7
Q: Cliff walking: what do SARSA and Q-learning learn, and why?
A: Reward -1 per step, -100 and back to start if you step into the cliff.
- **Q-learning** learns the **optimal path** right along the cliff edge, because its target assumes greedy behaviour. But with $\varepsilon$-greedy exploration it occasionally falls off, so its **online reward during training is worse**.
- **SARSA** learns the **safer path** further from the edge, because its values include the risk of its own random exploratory steps.
If $\varepsilon$ decays to 0, both converge to the optimal path. Use SARSA when mistakes during learning are costly (real robots).
K: cliff walking, SARSA, Q-learning, safe exploration

@card w7
Q: What is Expected SARSA and how does it relate to SARSA and Q-learning?
A: Target: $R_{t+1} + \gamma \sum_a \pi(a\mid S_{t+1}) Q(S_{t+1},a)$, the expectation over the next action instead of a sample.
- Removes the variance of sampling $A_{t+1}$, so it is more stable, especially at large $\alpha$.
- More computation per step.
- If the target policy $\pi$ is greedy, the expectation equals the max, so Expected SARSA **is** Q-learning. It generalises both.
K: Expected SARSA, variance reduction, generalisation of Q-learning

@card w7
Q: What is maximisation bias and how does Double Q-learning fix it?
A: If several actions have true value 0 but noisy estimates, $\max_a Q(s,a)$ will usually be positive, because the max picks whichever estimate happens to be too high. Q-learning uses that max in its target, so it **overestimates**.
**Double Q-learning** keeps $Q_1$ and $Q_2$. With probability 0.5:
$$Q_1(S,A) \leftarrow Q_1 + \alpha[R + \gamma Q_2(S', \arg\max_a Q_1(S',a)) - Q_1(S,A)]$$
and vice versa. One table **selects** the action, the other **evaluates** it, so the same noise cannot both choose and confirm an action. Double DQN applies the same idea with the online and target networks.
K: maximisation bias, overestimation, Double Q-learning, Double DQN

@card w7
Q: Unify DP, MC and TD. What are the two axes?
A: Axis 1, **depth**: TD backs up one step, MC backs up to the end of the episode, n-step in between.
Axis 2, **width**: sample backups (one sampled successor: MC, TD) vs full backups (all successors weighted by probability: DP, exhaustive search).
- DP: full and shallow, needs a model.
- TD: sample and shallow, model-free, bootstraps.
- MC: sample and deep, model-free, no bootstrapping.
- Exhaustive search: full and deep.
K: unified view, backup depth, sample backup, full backup

@card w7
Q: What are afterstates and when are they useful?
A: An **afterstate** is the situation right after your own move but before the environment or opponent responds, like a tic-tac-toe board after you place your mark.
Useful when the immediate effect of your action is known even if the rest of the dynamics is not. Different (position, move) pairs that lead to the same board share one value, so learning is faster. Also used in queuing problems.
K: afterstate, post-decision state, tic-tac-toe

@card w7
Q: What conditions guarantee tabular Q-learning converges to q-star?
A: - Every state-action pair is visited infinitely often (the behaviour policy keeps exploring).
- Step sizes satisfy the Robbins-Monro conditions $\sum_t \alpha_t = \infty$ and $\sum_t \alpha_t^2 < \infty$ (for example $\alpha_t = 1/N(s,a)$).
- Finite MDP and bounded rewards.
With a constant $\alpha$ it converges to a neighbourhood that jitters. With function approximation the guarantee disappears (deadly triad).
K: convergence, Robbins-Monro, infinite exploration

@card w8
Q: Why do tabular methods fail on real problems?
A: - **Memory**: Backgammon about $10^{20}$ states, Go about $10^{170}$, helicopter control is continuous.
- **Time and data**: every state must be visited many times before its entry is any good.
- **No generalisation**: learning about one state teaches nothing about a similar one. If the state is an image you cannot even list the states.
The real challenge is **generalisation**. Function approximation answers it.
K: tabular, curse of dimensionality, generalisation, function approximation

@card w8
Q: What is value function approximation and what are the three ways to set up the approximator?
A: Replace the table with $\hat v(s,\mathbf w) \approx v_\pi(s)$ or $\hat q(s,a,\mathbf w) \approx q_\pi(s,a)$, where the weight vector $\mathbf w$ is far smaller than the number of states. Train $\mathbf w$ with MC or TD targets using SGD.
Three architectures:
1. State in, $\hat v(s)$ out (prediction).
2. State and action in, one $\hat q(s,a)$ out.
3. State in, one $\hat q(s,a)$ per action out. Efficient for a small discrete action set because one forward pass gives every action value. DQN uses this.
K: VFA, weights, architectures, generalisation

@card w8
Q: Which function approximators can you use, and how do you choose?
A: - **Tables**: exact, no generalisation, do not scale.
- **Linear in features** $\mathbf w^\top \mathbf x(s)$: strong convergence guarantees, cheap, but needs good hand-made features (tile coding, RBF, Fourier, polynomial).
- **Neural networks / CNNs**: learn their own features from raw inputs like pixels, powerful, weakest theory.
- Decision trees, nearest neighbour, kernels (nonparametric).
Choice: small low-dimensional continuous state, use linear with tile coding. Raw images, use a CNN. Whatever you pick must handle **non-stationary, incrementally arriving data**, which is what RL produces.
K: function approximator, linear, neural network, tile coding, non-stationary

@card w8
Q: What is the VE objective and why do we need a state weighting?
A: $\overline{VE}(\mathbf w) = \sum_s \mu(s)[v_\pi(s) - \hat v(s,\mathbf w)]^2$.
With fewer weights than states, improving one state's estimate makes others worse, so you must decide which states matter. $\mu(s)$, the **on-policy distribution** (fraction of time spent in $s$), weights errors by how often you are actually there. Semi-gradient TD's convergence results depend on updating according to this distribution.
K: VE, mean squared value error, on-policy distribution

@card w8
Q: Gradient MC vs semi-gradient TD. Why is TD only a semi-gradient?
A: General update: $\mathbf w \leftarrow \mathbf w + \alpha[U_t - \hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)]\nabla_{\mathbf w}\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)$.
- **Gradient MC**: $U_t = G_t$, unbiased and independent of $\mathbf w$. True SGD on VE. Converges to a local optimum (global for linear).
- **Semi-gradient TD(0)**: $U_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma\hat v(S_{t+1},\mathbf w)$. The target depends on $\mathbf w$, but we ignore that dependence and differentiate only $\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w)$. So it is not the gradient of any fixed objective.
Semi-gradient TD learns faster and online, converges for linear on-policy (to the TD fixed point), but can diverge off-policy or with nonlinear nets.
K: gradient MC, semi-gradient, TD target, SGD

@card w8
Q: Linear value function approximation: write it, its update, and its convergence result.
A: $\hat v(s,\mathbf w) = \mathbf w^\top \mathbf x(s)$, so $\nabla_{\mathbf w}\hat v = \mathbf x(s)$ and the update is $\mathbf w \leftarrow \mathbf w + \alpha[U_t - \mathbf w^\top\mathbf x(S_t)]\,\mathbf x(S_t)$.
Update equals step size times prediction error times feature value.
- Gradient MC converges to the **global** optimum of VE (linear has one optimum).
- Linear semi-gradient TD(0) converges to the **TD fixed point** $\mathbf w_{TD} = A^{-1}\mathbf b$, with $\overline{VE}(\mathbf w_{TD}) \le \frac{1}{1-\gamma}\min_{\mathbf w}\overline{VE}(\mathbf w)$.
Your notes write the update with a minus sign in sections 18 and 19. With error defined as target minus prediction, the correct sign is plus. Do not repeat the minus in the defense.
K: linear FA, feature vector, TD fixed point, convergence

@card w8
Q: Explain coarse coding and tile coding. Why is tile coding popular?
A: **Coarse coding**: cover the state space with overlapping regions. A state's feature is 1 for each region it falls in. Two states generalise in proportion to the regions they share. Bigger regions generalise more broadly early on, but final resolution depends mostly on how many features you have.
**Tile coding**: several offset grids (**tilings**). Each state activates exactly **one tile per tiling**, so the number of active features is constant. That makes step size easy to set ($\alpha = \frac{1}{10n}$ for $n$ tilings is a common start) and computation cheap (sum $n$ weights). Asymmetric offsets avoid diagonal artefacts. **Hashing** cuts memory.
Classic use: Mountain Car with semi-gradient SARSA.
K: coarse coding, tile coding, tilings, hashing, Mountain Car

@card w8
Q: What are polynomial, Fourier and RBF features?
A: - **Polynomial**: $(1, s_1, s_2, s_1 s_2, \dots)$. The constant allows an offset, the product captures interaction. Generalise poorly online at high order.
- **Fourier basis**: $x_i(s) = \cos(\pi\,\mathbf c_i^\top \mathbf s)$. Different $\mathbf c$ give different frequencies and interactions. Use smaller step sizes for high frequencies.
- **RBF**: $x_i(s) = \exp\left(-\frac{\lVert s - c_i\rVert^2}{2\sigma_i^2}\right)$, a smooth bump around centre $c_i$. Smooth and differentiable, but more costly than tile coding in high dimensions.
K: polynomial features, Fourier basis, RBF, feature construction

@card w8
Q: What is state aggregation and what does it look like?
A: Group states, one shared weight per group. It is the simplest linear FA (a one-hot feature per group). On the 1000-state random walk with 10 groups, the estimate is a **staircase**: constant within groups, jumps between them, and the edge groups are pulled towards their most visited states because of $\mu$.
K: state aggregation, staircase, random walk

@card w8
Q: What is LSTD and when would you use it?
A: **Least-Squares TD** estimates the TD fixed point directly instead of creeping towards it: $\hat A = \sum_t \mathbf x_t(\mathbf x_t - \gamma\mathbf x_{t+1})^\top + \epsilon I$, $\hat{\mathbf b} = \sum_t R_{t+1}\mathbf x_t$, then $\mathbf w = \hat A^{-1}\hat{\mathbf b}$.
- Most **data efficient** linear TD method, no step size needed.
- Costs $O(d^2)$ per step with Sherman-Morrison updates of the inverse (vs $O(d)$ for TD).
Use it when data is expensive and the number of features $d$ is moderate.
K: LSTD, TD fixed point, data efficiency, Sherman-Morrison

@card w8
Q: What is the deadly triad?
A: Combining all three of:
1. **Function approximation**
2. **Bootstrapping** (TD-style targets)
3. **Off-policy training**
can make value estimates diverge to infinity. Any two are fine. Q-learning with a neural network (DQN) has all three, which is why DQN needs experience replay and a target network to stay stable in practice.
K: deadly triad, divergence, off-policy, bootstrapping, function approximation

@card w8
Q: What are memory-based and kernel-based methods?
A: **Memory-based (lazy)** methods store experiences and only compute when queried, for example nearest neighbour or a distance-weighted average of nearby stored targets. Nonparametric: the shape of the function is not fixed in advance and accuracy improves with data. They focus on visited regions and avoid exponential memory, but queries slow down as memory grows (k-d trees help).
**Kernel regression**: $\hat v(s) = \sum_{s'\in D} k(s,s')\,g(s')$, where the kernel measures how strongly $s'$ generalises to $s$. Any linear method corresponds to the kernel $k(s,s') = \mathbf x(s)^\top\mathbf x(s')$ (kernel trick).
K: memory-based, nonparametric, kernel, nearest neighbour

@card w8
Q: Describe Mountain Car and why it needs function approximation.
A: An underpowered car in a valley must reach the top of the right hill. It must first reverse up the left slope to build momentum. State: position and velocity, both **continuous**. Actions: reverse, none, forward. Reward -1 per step.
The continuous state rules out a table. Semi-gradient (n-step) SARSA with tile coding or RBF features learns the rocking strategy. The agent starts optimistic everywhere, which drives early exploration.
K: Mountain Car, continuous state, semi-gradient SARSA, tile coding

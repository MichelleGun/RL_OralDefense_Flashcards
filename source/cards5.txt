@card d
I: dq_loop
Q: Walk through exactly what happens in one control tick on the real drone.
A: Fifteen times a second:
1. **See.** The camera frame goes to an OpenCV ring detector (HSV colour mask, ellipse fit).
2. **Describe.** `ObsBuilder` packs detector outputs and telemetry into **41 numbers** (37 features plus a 4-number ring tracker), normalised to roughly $[-1, 1]$. A history keeps the frames 0, 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 ticks ago, so the actor sees **246 inputs**.
3. **Decide.** The actor network outputs 4 numbers in $[-1, 1]$: left/right, forward/back, up/down, yaw. At flight time it uses the **mean action** (deterministic, no exploration noise).
4. **Act.** Each number is multiplied by 30, rounded and sent with `send_rc_control`. Safety layers can trim it first (soft floor and ceiling, travel budget).
The observation builder and the actor are the same code in sim and on the drone. Everything outside them (camera, detector, physics) is where sim-to-real error lives.
K: control loop, observation, actor, 15 Hz, deployment

@card d
Q: Why does the policy see 41 hand-made features instead of the camera image?
A: Three reasons:
- **Compute.** Everything trains on a laptop CPU with no GPU. A 2 x 64 MLP on features runs millions of steps overnight. A CNN on rendered frames would need rendering in the simulator and far more samples.
- **Sim-to-real gap.** A pixel policy has to bridge the visual gap between rendered and real images. With features, the gap shrinks to "does the detector report the same numbers in both worlds".
- **Interpretability.** Each input has a meaning (range, axis ratio, age since last seen), so we could debug failures input by input.
The cost, and say it before they do: the policy can only know what the detector chose to measure. On real flights the detector reports the ring "overflowing the frame" close up, and the simulator's detector is clean geometry there. That gap is still open.
K: feature engineering, pixels vs features, CNN, sim-to-real

Q: Name the most important observation fields and say why each one exists.
A: - `found`: searching and approaching need opposite behaviour.
- `cx, cy`: where the ring is in the picture, so which way to turn and climb.
- `scale`, `range`: two distance cues, one raw, one computed from apparent size.
- `axis_ratio`: how round the ring looks. The **only** cue that the drone is off the ring's axis, which is needed to strafe into line.

@card d
Q: Is your problem an MDP? Defend your answer.
A: **No, it is a POMDP**, and that turned out to be the central difficulty.
- The ring looks the same from the front and the back.
- "Just went through, ring behind me" and "blind, about to search" produce the same camera features: ring not in view.

@card d
I: dq_aac
Q: Explain the ring tracker. Why is it a belief state and how accurate is it?
A: A **belief state** is a best guess of what the agent cannot see (Sutton and Barto 17.3). The tracker is written from geometry, not learned:
- At each fresh detection, the ring's position in the drone frame comes from the camera: forward = range, right = $c_x \tan(35^\circ) \cdot$ range, up = $-c_y \tan(35^\circ) \cdot$ range.
- While the ring is out of view, the estimate is moved by **dead reckoning**: telemetry yaw for turns, height for climbs, executed commands times nominal speed for forward and sideways.
- It adds 4 inputs (forward, right, up, seen yet), taking 37 to 41.
Accuracy in sim: median 2 cm in view, 7 cm after 1 to 2 s blind, **19 cm after more than 3 s blind**. If the real forward speed is 1.5 times the assumed one, error at 2 to 3 s blind rises to 18 cm.
Weakness: forward speed per stick command has never been measured on the real Tello, so the tracker's odometry rests on an assumed constant.
K: ring tracker, belief state, dead reckoning, POMDP

@card d
I: dq_aac
Q: Why a frame history with lags 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 instead of an LSTM or stacking 16 frames?
A: - **Why memory at all:** it makes "I just went through, the ring was ahead a moment ago" visible, so after-the-pass states stop looking like before-the-pass states.
- **Why these lags:** log-spaced lags cover about 1 s (16 ticks at 15 Hz) with only 6 frames. Dense stacking of 16 frames would give 656 inputs with mostly redundant neighbours.
- **Why not an LSTM:** a recurrent policy learns its own memory, but recurrent PPO needs more data, is harder to train stably and harder to export. The stack plus tracker writes the memory we already know we need.
- **Course link:** this is the Atari frame-stacking idea (one frame does not show motion), scaled to a longer horizon.
Limitation: 1 s of history is short compared with a 10 to 15 s lap. The tracker covers the longer blind periods, with drifting error.
K: frame stacking, history, LSTM, recurrent policy

@card d
I: dq_aac
Q: Describe your network architecture precisely.
A: Stable-Baselines3 `ActorCriticPolicy` with **separate** actor and critic networks (no shared layers):
- **Actor:** 246 inputs (41 features x 6 lags) through two hidden layers of 64 with **tanh**, then a linear layer to the 4 action means. A **diagonal Gaussian** policy with a learned, state-independent `log_std` per channel. No squashing: actions outside $[-1, 1]$ are clipped.
- **Critic:** the same 246 inputs **plus 20 privileged simulator values** (266 in total), two layers of 64, tanh, linear to one value $V(s)$.
- The asymmetric part is done by a `MaskExtractor` that multiplies the privileged columns by zero for the actor, so on the drone those inputs can be zeros.
- Initialisation orthogonal, optimiser Adam (SB3 defaults).
Be honest that 2 x 64 and tanh were SB3 defaults, never tuned for this task.
K: architecture, MLP, actor-critic, Gaussian policy, tanh

@card d
I: dq_aac
Q: What is an asymmetric actor-critic and why is it legitimate?
A: The **critic** sees privileged simulator state (true ring position and similar, 20 values). The **actor** sees only what the real drone can measure.
Why it is legitimate:
- The critic only exists during training. It is used to compute advantages, never to choose actions, so the deployed policy needs nothing the drone lacks.
- In a POMDP, the camera features alone give a noisy value estimate. A better value estimate means **lower-variance advantages**, so a cleaner policy gradient.
- A baseline that depends on the state but not the action does not bias the policy gradient in expectation.
Caveat to state: with GAE $\lambda = 0.95$ the advantage also **bootstraps** from $V$, so critic errors can add some bias, not just noise. Reference idea: Pinto et al. 2017, asymmetric actor-critic for sim-to-real.
K: asymmetric critic, privileged information, variance reduction, baseline

@card d
Q: What is the action space, and why velocity sticks instead of position targets or motor speeds?
A: 4 continuous numbers in $[-1, 1]$: lr, fb, ud, yaw. Each is scaled to a Tello remote-control stick value (x 30), which the Tello's own flight controller turns into velocity.
- **Why not motor RPMs:** the real Tello does not accept them. The policy must output what the hardware accepts.
- **Why not position targets:** in stage 2, position targets (`pid`) made the drone tip over within about 8 control steps every episode, 0/20 reached. Bounded velocity commands (`vel`) reached 9/20.
- **Why x 30 (30 % stick):** a deliberate safety limit, about 0.24 m/s at full command in the sim.
- **Why yaw is a channel:** the policy must turn to face the ring plane. In the old point-reaching task yaw was held by a PD loop, so the policy could never orient.
Continuous actions are also why DQN does not fit without a discretising wrapper.
K: action space, continuous control, velocity commands, safety

@card d
Q: Why PPO? Why not SAC, TD3 or DQN?
A: - **Continuous 4D actions:** rules out plain DQN (it needs a max over actions).
- **Cheap parallel simulation:** our simulator runs many copies on a CPU, so sample efficiency matters less than stability. PPO's on-policy batches from 4 to 8 environments suit that.
- **Stability:** the clipped objective plus our `target_kl` brake stopped the collapses we saw early on.
- **Imitation term:** adding a behaviour cloning loss to PPO (DAPG) is simple. Mixing demos into an off-policy replay buffer is messier.
- **Tooling:** SB3's PPO is mature, and the course covered it in Week 10.
SAC would be the natural rival (off-policy, entropy-regularised exploration, which we lacked). Honesty point: the dashboard can train A2C, SAC, TD3 and DQN, but if we never ran a seeded head-to-head on the onboard task, say that clearly instead of claiming PPO is "best".
K: PPO, SAC, TD3, DQN, algorithm choice

@card d
Q: Summarise the PPO update in your own words, including your extra terms.
A: 1. Collect 2048 steps per environment with the current stochastic policy.
2. Compute advantages with **GAE** ($\gamma = 0.99$, $\lambda = 0.95$): how much better each action turned out than the critic expected.
3. Maximise the **clipped surrogate** $\mathbb{E}[\min(r_t A_t, \text{clip}(r_t, 0.8, 1.2) A_t)]$ with $r_t = \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) / \pi_{old}(a_t|s_t)$, over 5 epochs of batches of 512.
4. Stop the epochs early if the approximate KL exceeds `target_kl` (0.01). This is the **trust-region brake**.
5. Train the critic by regression on returns.
6. **DAPG term:** after each PPO update, take behaviour cloning steps that pull the actor's mean towards DAgger teacher labels, coefficient **0.3, kept constant**.
So the actor follows the RL gradient and a supervised pull at the same time.
K: PPO, GAE, clipped objective, target KL, DAPG

@card d
Q: At 15 Hz, is $\gamma = 0.99$ long enough for a lap that takes 10 to 15 seconds?
A: Effective horizon $\approx 1/(1-\gamma) = 100$ ticks $\approx 6.7$ s. A reward one lap away (about 150 to 225 ticks) is weighted $0.99^{150} \approx 0.22$ to $0.99^{225} \approx 0.10$. So the next pass is nearly invisible to the critic.
We tested $\gamma = 0.995$ (horizon about 13 s) several times and it did not help. Plausible reasons:
- Longer horizons raise the variance of returns and make the critic harder to fit.
- The real bottleneck was partial observability, not the horizon. Once the policy had memory, laps improved at $\gamma = 0.99$.
- The streak count in the observation and potential-based shaping give the critic nearer-term signals.
Admit the remaining risk: we did not retest $\gamma = 0.995$ on the memory policy.
K: discount factor, horizon, credit assignment

Q: Walk through the reward function of the current policy.
A: Per tick unless stated:
- **Path shaping** 3 per metre of progress towards a point 0.3 m in front of the ring, then 0.3 m behind it (so stopping in the opening loses reward).
- **Axis pull** 1 x off-axis metres near the plane, **facing** 0.5, **time** $-0.02$, **dwell** $-0.05$ for loitering in the opening, **rim** up to $-0.3$ within 8 cm of the noodle.
- **Pass** +20 x streak multiplier $\min(1 + 0.5(k-1), 3)$, only camera-first and in the right direction.
- **Wrong way** back through the opening $-20$. **Strike** $-10$, **out of cage** $-10$, **miss** $-5$, all ending the episode. **Found** +0.5 the first time the ring comes into view.
Design logic: the large terminal penalties make crashing worse than flying on, the streak makes consecutive laps worth more, and the cap at 3 stops one episode's return from exploding the critic's scale.
K: reward design, shaping, streak bonus, penalties

@card d
Q: Your shaping is called potential-based. Is all of your reward shaping actually policy-invariant?
A: Only part of it, and saying so is stronger than overclaiming.
- **Ng, Harada and Russell (1999):** adding $F = \gamma \Phi(s') - \Phi(s)$ leaves the optimal policy unchanged. Our path term pays progress towards waypoints, which is of that form (strictly, the $\gamma$ should multiply $\Phi(s')$, so with $\gamma < 1$ the match is approximate). Circling or hovering earns nothing net, which is exactly why it fixed v2's parking.
- **Not potential-based:** axis pull, facing, rim, dwell and time terms. These are ordinary per-state rewards and **can change the optimal policy**. They were added on purpose as priors, for example "line up before going in".
- Evidence they bite: the stronger rim and axis terms (run OZ, LZ) made the policy more cautious and it passed less.
K: potential-based shaping, policy invariance, Ng 1999

@card d
Q: Why does touching the net end the episode with a "drain" penalty, and why does wrong-way cost so much?
A: - **Net ends the episode:** the real drone has to land when it hits the net, so the sim must not let it bounce and continue.
- **Drain** (in the budget versions): with a flat small penalty, a drone that cannot pass would learn to **dive into the net** to stop paying the time cost. Draining the remaining budget makes ending early never cheaper than flying on. (The current recipe sets `drain` off and uses large fixed terminal penalties instead, $-10$.)
- **Wrong way $-20$:** without it, the cheapest "lap" is to dither back and forth through the opening. A strong penalty forces the intended behaviour: go round the outside and re-enter from the same side.
This is the same principle as v2's parking: penalties must make the unintended shortcut strictly worse than the intended behaviour.
K: termination, penalties, reward hacking, drain

@card d
Q: Why does every episode start with the drone already hovering at about 0.8 m?
A: Because that is where control is handed over on the real drone. The Tello's motors only start with the firmware `takeoff` command, which climbs to about 0.7 to 0.8 m by itself, and `fly_hoop.py` hands over to the policy after a 2 s settle.
- Learning take-off in simulation would train a skill the real drone never uses.
- PyBullet's Crazyflie take-off does not resemble the Tello's anyway.
- Robustness comes from **where** control is handed over: start height 0.7 to 0.9 m, start zone cycled through 9 zones, random facing.
Landing is never learned either. It is scripted for safety (keys, `land.flag`, airborne time limit, battery, temperature, height window).
K: initial state distribution, takeoff, deployment, safety

@card d
Q: What role does imitation learning play, and how did it fail at first?
A: Three kinds of demonstrations, each with a lesson:
- **Behaviour cloning of a scripted expert:** the expert passes 100 % in sim, but the clone scored only **6/12 against the real detector**. Classic covariate shift: small errors take it to states the expert never visited.
- **Human keyboard flights:** they never loop. Imitating them pulled the policy towards "pass once, then stop". Removing that imitation term (run W) took laps from 0.16 to **0.45**.
- **DAgger with a privileged teacher:** the teacher sees the true sim state and labels the right action on the states the student actually visits, fixing covariate shift.
DAgger alone stayed at 0 to 0.11 laps because imitation is not precise enough: the student's actions differed by about 0.2 from the teacher's, against 8 cm of margin. So imitation provides the coarse behaviour, RL provides the precision.
K: behaviour cloning, DAgger, covariate shift, imitation learning

@card d
Q: Explain the "averaging two good choices" trap you hit with DAgger.
A: Each episode starts with the ring out of view. The privileged teacher knows where the ring is and turns the right way at once, sometimes left, sometimes right. The camera-only student cannot know which way, so regression on mean squared error learned the **average of left and right**: it flew straight into the net within 6 s without ever seeing the ring (4 of 6 episodes).
Fix: **search-first labels**. Until the student's camera has seen the ring, the teacher's label is a fixed search the student can reproduce (turn clockwise and climb to ring height). The honest teacher still flies 4.1 laps per 90 s.
General rule: a privileged teacher must not use information the student can never have, or the student learns the average over what it cannot distinguish. Same trap as the memoryless loop labels.
K: DAgger, privileged teacher, multimodal actions, MSE averaging

@card d
Q: Is your final agent really reinforcement learning, or is the teacher doing the work?
A: Both, and the split is measurable:
- The DAgger clone with memory gives PPO a start that **loops sometimes**. Without it, nothing looped.

@card d
Q: What does the 0.3 behaviour cloning term do to the objective theoretically?
A: The actor now minimises $L = L_{PPO} + 0.3 \cdot \mathbb{E}_{(s, a^*) \sim \mathcal{D}} \|\mu_\theta(s) - a^*\|^2$.
- It is an **off-policy** pull. The labels come from DAgger rounds on older student states, so they do not match the current policy's state distribution.
- It biases the solution away from the return-maximising policy wherever the teacher is suboptimal for the camera-only agent.
- Benefit: it acts like a KL anchor to a known-good behaviour, the same idea as the KL penalty to a reference model in RLHF. It keeps the loop behaviour from being forgotten while PPO chases precision.
- Also: the BC loss only shapes the **mean**. Exploration noise comes from `log_std`, which BC does not touch.
K: DAPG, off-policy regularisation, KL anchor, RLHF analogy

@card d
Q: Your exploration noise collapsed. What does that mean and what did you do?
A: The policy's Gaussian noise is $\sigma = e^{\text{log\_std}}$. In every long run `log_std` reached about $-2.3$ to $-2.8$, so $\sigma \approx 0.06$ to $0.10$ of full stick. With `ent_coef = 0`, nothing pushes back.
Consequence: the policy can barely discover new behaviour, which fitted runs getting worse with more training.
What we tried: an entropy bonus 0.005 plus resetting `log_std` to $-1$ (run D), and gSDE state-dependent exploration (run E). Neither beat the trust-region run C in the race.
What we did instead: change the **start-state distribution** (slanted starts, loop-practice starts) and **dynamics randomisation**. Those inject variety through the environment instead of action noise.
Honest point: low $\sigma$ also makes training data nearly deterministic, a reason the gains per round have shrunk.
K: exploration, entropy, log std, gSDE

I: dq_lineage
Q: Explain your automated optimiser and how it decides a new best policy.
A: `loop_forever.py` repeats rounds: take the best policy so far (the parent), train two copies for 2M more steps (the parent's own recipe and one new idea), re-score everything on a fixed referee, keep the winner.
- **Referee:** the same **72 laps episodes plus 60 slanted starts** for every candidate, score = laps per episode + 1.0 x oblique entry.
- **Margin:** a challenger must win by **more than 0.10**, because one 36-episode evaluation wobbles by about $\pm 0.15$ laps.
- **Seeds:** each round uses its own seed. Rounds 4 and 5 used the same seed and trained the identical policy (both 2.155), a wasted round.
- Earlier rounds used a **successive-halving race**: stop a run once it falls 0.10 below the reference curve at the same step.
Lineage of winners: n8 1.28, O 1.58, E9 2.00, C9 2.33, CL 2.58, LO 4.33, **LE 5.29** laps per episode.
K: hyperparameter search, successive halving, model selection, optimiser

@card d
Q: The examiner says: "You picked the best of many noisy runs. Your reported score is inflated." How do you respond?
A: Agree that the risk is real. This is **maximisation bias**, the same thing as the max in Q-learning: the maximum of noisy estimates is biased upwards (winner's curse).
What we did against it:
- Every candidate, including the parent, is **re-scored** on the same 72 plus 60 episodes, not taken from its own training evaluation.
- A challenger needs a **margin of 0.10**, about the measured noise level.
- The re-score and the export agree closely (LE: 5.29 on 72 episodes vs 5.33 on 36).
What we did not do: a fully **held-out test** on new layouts and seeds after selection, which is the Double Q-learning idea applied to model selection (select on one set, report on another). Also `best_model.zip` inside a run is still picked on 36 evaluation episodes. With more time, report every finalist on a fresh test set with confidence intervals.
K: selection bias, maximisation bias, held-out test, winner's curse

- **Repeated parent continuations:** each round retrains the parent's recipe with a new seed, which shows run-to-run spread (for example C9 retrained with seeds 9, 10 and 12 reached referee scores of 2.35, 2.55 and 2.60).
- **Measured evaluation noise:** about $\pm 0.15$ laps per episode for a 36-episode evaluation.
What a strict examiner wants: the final recipe trained from the same parent with 3 to 5 seeds, plotted as mean and band, and evaluated on held-out layouts. Name that as the first thing you would add.
K: seeds, reproducibility, variance, evaluation

@card d
Q: Explain the hierarchical RL experiment and why it did not beat the flat policy.
A: **Options framework** (Sutton, Precup and Singh 1999):
- Skills: **SE** (enter cleanly, trained on slanted starts and episodes ending at the first pass), **SL** (loop, every episode starts just before the hoop), plus the flat **C9** generalist.
- **Manager:** PPO over the 3 options, deciding every 8 ticks (0.53 s) or at a pass. A semi-MDP with discount $0.92 \approx 0.99^8$ per decision.
- Baseline: a **fixed switch** (enter until the first pass, then loop).
Result: flat C9 **2.75**, learned manager 2.74, fixed switch 2.51. The manager picked C9 about 90 % of the time.
Why: the skills had **2M steps** of training against C9's **about 30M**, so the comparison was unfair to the specialists. The manager correctly learned that the generalist was the best skill available. Parked, would need equally trained skills.
K: hierarchical RL, options, semi-MDP, manager

@card d
Q: How do you guarantee that the policy evaluated in the sim is exactly the policy that flies?
A: An export pipeline with checks:
- Policies load through `policy.load_policy`, the same loader `fly_hoop.py` uses: same yaw convention, same input width, same history stack, privileged inputs filled with zeros.
- The sim score is computed through that loader's `policy_fn` on observations from `ObsBuilder`, the class the flight uses.
- The exported copy in `hardware/policies/` is reloaded and must produce the **same actions on 200 recorded observations**.
- File names carry run, checkpoint and score, and each flight's `meta.json` records which model flew.
- Old policies with fewer inputs are **widened** with zero weights on new inputs, so they behave identically before further training.
- The hierarchy has a tick-for-tick test that the flight version sends the same sticks as in sim.
K: deployment, export, reproducibility, testing

@card d
Q: What safety design surrounds a learned policy on a real drone?
A: The policy is never trusted alone:
- **Take-off and landing are scripted**, never learned.
- **Height window:** soft floor at 40 cm (no further descent, forced climb 10 cm lower), soft ceiling within 25 cm of the maximum, hard abort near the floor.
- **Travel budget** on the integral of forward commands, **airborne time limit**, **battery and temperature** limits (80 C).
- **Human override:** Ctrl+C, L or Esc to land, an emergency motor cut, `land.flag` from the dashboard.
- **Staged flights:** dry run in the simulated drone, hover test, alignment only, bounded approach, then full flight.
- The dashboard is never exposed beyond localhost because it can fly a real drone.
Trade-off to admit: safety trims change the executed action, so the real closed loop differs slightly from training. Trims are logged so they can be found.
K: safety, deployment, robotics, override

Q: Which course topics does your project use? Map them week by week.
A: - **W3 MDPs and W1 framing:** state, action, reward, termination design, and recognising the task is a **POMDP**.
- **W4 Bellman / value functions:** the critic $V(s)$ and the advantage $A = Q - V$.
- **W7 TD:** GAE bootstraps with TD errors $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$, blended by $\lambda$.
- **W8 function approximation:** MLP actor and critic, feature engineering, normalised inputs.
- **W9 deep RL:** frame stacking for partial observability, stability tricks, maximisation bias in model selection.
- **W10 policy gradients:** Gaussian policy, actor-critic, PPO clipping, trust regions via `target_kl`, entropy.
- **W11 planning / RLHF ideas:** KL-style anchoring to a reference behaviour (the BC term), and hierarchical options.
- **Exploration vs exploitation:** collapsed $\sigma$, start-state curricula.

Q: What are the biggest limitations of your project? Name them before the examiner does.
A: 1. **Real-world evidence is thin.** The policies that loop 5+ times are simulation results. The real drone entered from one corner and clipped from the other three.
2. **Unmeasured physics.** Forward speed per command, camera field of view and close-range detector behaviour are assumptions. The tracker and `pushed` both depend on the forward speed.
3. **Close-range perception gap.** The real detector reports frame overflow and noisy ellipses near the ring. The simulator's detector is clean.
4. **Corner entry stalled** around 60 % while laps rose.
5. **Statistics.** Mostly one seed per configuration, no held-out test after selection, model choice on 36 to 72 episodes.
6. **No pure-RL ablation** of the constant BC term on the memory policy, and no fair comparison against SAC.
7. **Plateau.** No challenger has beaten LE since round 21 (about 10 rounds).
K: limitations, honesty, sim-to-real, statistics

@card d
Q: The examiner says: "This is massively over-engineered. Why not a scripted visual-servo controller?"
A: We built one, and it is a useful baseline.
- The scripted expert passes **100 %** from the front runway on the typical profile and 60/60 with the real detector.
- But on the **measured** dynamics it passes only **3 %**: its small yaw corrections fall inside the real yaw dead zone, so it never finishes lining up.
- From hard starts (behind, beside, below) it manages 0 to 33 %, and it scores **0.3 laps per 90 s**.
- Writing a hand controller for search, line-up, pass, go round, re-acquire and re-enter, robust to drift, delay and a moving ring, is exactly the brittle part.
So RL earns its place on the looping and robustness, not on a single straight pass. Concede that for one front pass from a known start, a scripted controller is simpler and was better.
K: baseline, scripted controller, justification

@card d
Q: How did training infrastructure choices affect your results?
A: - **Parallel evaluation** cut wall-clock time (evaluation was about 60 % of it) but made evaluation numbers about $\pm 0.15$ noisy across copy counts, which is why the referee re-scores everything identically.
- **Checkpoints** every evaluation and every 15 min, crashed runs resume from their last save. Sleep, out-of-memory and a stray Ctrl+C each killed runs before we fixed them (detached processes, memory checks).
- **ClearML** logs every run and flight, so hyperparameters and curves can be compared.
- **Continuation chains:** almost every model continues from a parent, so the step count is cumulative (LE is about 30M steps). That means results are path-dependent: a different early choice could have led elsewhere.
Lesson: in RL the experiment harness decides how trustworthy the numbers are.
K: infrastructure, evaluation noise, checkpoints, path dependence

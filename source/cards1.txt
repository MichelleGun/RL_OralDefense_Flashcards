Q: Define reinforcement learning in one sentence, then say what makes it different from supervised learning.
P: Think of training a puppy. Nobody gives it a list of correct answers. It tries something, gets a treat or nothing, and slowly works out which behaviour earns treats. That is reinforcement learning: learning by trial and error from rewards.
Supervised learning is like studying with an answer key. Every example comes with the right answer. In RL there is no answer key, only a score that may come much later.
A: **Reinforcement learning** is learning to make a good **sequence of decisions** by interacting with an **environment** and receiving **scalar reward** feedback.
Differences from supervised learning:
- No labels. The agent is never told the correct action, it only gets a reward that says how good its choice was.
- **Delayed consequences**. A reward now may be caused by an action many steps ago (the **credit assignment** problem).
- The agent's own actions decide what data it sees next, so it must **explore**. In supervised learning the dataset is fixed.
- Data is sequential and correlated, not independent and identically distributed.
K: reward, sequential decisions, exploration, delayed reward, credit assignment

Q: When would you choose supervised learning, unsupervised learning, imitation learning or RL?
P: Ask two questions. Do I have the right answers written down? Then use supervised learning, like a student with a marked practice exam. Do I have no answers and just want to find groups or patterns? Then use unsupervised learning, like sorting a pile of photos into piles that look alike.
If an expert can show you what to do, copy them (imitation learning, like learning to cook by watching a chef). If nobody knows the best move and your choices change what happens next, you have to learn by trying (RL).
A: - **Supervised**: you have labelled data and a one-shot prediction (classify a shark image).
- **Unsupervised**: no labels, you want structure in the data (cluster customers).
- **Imitation learning**: you have expert **demonstrations** of good behaviour and want to copy them. Weakness: it can never be better than the demonstrations and fails in situations they never covered.
- **RL**: the task is a sequence of decisions, feedback is a reward that may be delayed, and the agent must explore to discover good behaviour.
Quick test in an exam: ask "is there a label for the right answer?" and "do my actions change what I see next?" If no label and yes, it is RL.
K: supervised, unsupervised, imitation learning, demonstrations

Q: Name the components of an RL system and describe the agent environment loop.
P: Picture a video game. You are the **agent**, the game world is the **environment**. Each moment you look at the screen (the **state**), press a button (the **action**), and the game gives you points (the **reward**) and a new screen.
The **policy** is your playing style, the rule for which button you press on each screen. The **value function** is your gut feeling for how good the current situation is. A **model**, if you have one, is your mental picture of how the game will react.
A: Components: **agent**, **environment**, **state**, **action**, **reward**, **policy** (and often a **value function** and a **model**).
Loop at each time step $t$:
1. The agent observes state $S_t$.
2. Its policy picks action $A_t$.
3. The environment transitions to $S_{t+1}$ and emits reward $R_{t+1}$.
4. The agent updates its policy or values and repeats.
In an MDP the transition probabilities and rewards are stored in the environment. The policy is stored in the agent. Examiners like that distinction.
K: agent, environment, state, action, reward, policy

Q: What is the exploration exploitation trade-off and why can't you just exploit?
P: Choosing where to eat tonight. **Exploiting** means going to your favourite restaurant because you know it is good. **Exploring** means trying a new place that might be even better, or might be worse.

P: **Model-based** is like a chess player who imagines moves in their head before playing. They have a mental model of what will happen. **Model-free** is like learning to ride a bike. You never compute physics, you just try, fall, and adjust.

Q: What is the difference between history, environment state, agent state and a Markov state?
P: The **history** is the full diary of everything you have seen and done. The **environment state** is the full truth about the world, which you usually cannot see, like the cards in other players' hands.
The **agent state** is your own summary, the notes you keep from the diary. A **Markov state** is a summary good enough that reading the old diary would not help you predict anything better. In chess, the current board is Markov. You do not need to know how the pieces got there.
A: - **History** $H_t$: everything observed so far: observations, actions, rewards.
- **Environment state**: the environment's private representation used to produce the next observation and reward. Usually invisible to the agent.
- **Agent state** $S^a_t = f(H_t)$: what the agent uses to choose actions.
- **Markov (information) state**: a state that keeps all useful information from the history, $P[S_{t+1}\mid S_t] = P[S_{t+1}\mid S_1,\dots,S_t]$.
You can always make a Markov state by using the whole history, but small states are cheaper in memory, computation and data.
K: history, agent state, environment state, Markov state

@card w2
I: egreedy
Q: Explain epsilon-greedy precisely. What happens with epsilon = 0 and epsilon = 1?
P: **Epsilon-greedy** is a coin flip before every choice. Most of the time (probability $1-\varepsilon$) you pick what looks best. Once in a while (probability $\varepsilon$) you pick something at random, just to check.
With $\varepsilon = 0$ you never check, so you can get stuck with a wrong favourite forever. With $\varepsilon = 1$ you always pick randomly and never use what you learned.
A: With probability $\varepsilon$ choose an action **uniformly at random** (explore). With probability $1-\varepsilon$ choose $\arg\max_a Q(a)$ (exploit). After each reward update $Q(A) \leftarrow Q(A) + \frac{1}{N(A)}(R - Q(A))$.
- $\varepsilon = 0$: pure greedy. Can get stuck on a suboptimal arm forever.
- $\varepsilon = 1$: pure random. Learns estimates but never uses them.
Subtle point: the random pick can also land on the greedy arm, so the greedy arm is chosen with probability $1 - \varepsilon + \varepsilon/k$.
You lost marks on this in a quiz by describing pure greedy. Say "random action with probability epsilon" first, every time.
K: epsilon-greedy, exploration, greedy, incremental update

@card w2
I: ucb
Q: Explain UCB. Why is it often better than epsilon-greedy?
P: **UCB** is "optimism under uncertainty". Picture rating restaurants. A place you visited once with one good meal could be great or average, you are unsure. UCB gives that place a bonus for being uncertain and tries it, then the bonus shrinks as you learn more.
Epsilon-greedy explores blindly, even trying places it already knows are bad. UCB explores where it is actually unsure, so it wastes fewer tries.
A: **Upper Confidence Bound**: pick $A_t = \arg\max_a \left[ Q(a) + c\sqrt{\frac{\ln t}{N(a)}} \right]$.
- First term: exploitation, the current estimate.
- Second term: exploration bonus. It is large when $N(a)$ is small (uncertain arm) and grows slowly with time through $\ln t$, so neglected arms are eventually retried.
- $c$ controls how much exploration you want.
Why better: $\varepsilon$-greedy explores blindly, wasting pulls on arms already known to be bad. UCB explores **in a directed way**, towards arms that might be optimal given their uncertainty ("optimism in the face of uncertainty").
In the lab code `np.sqrt(2*np.log(step+1)/(Num_pulls+1e-5))` the 1e-5 just avoids division by zero.
K: UCB, optimism, exploration bonus, uncertainty

Q: State the Markov property in words and in maths. Why does RL rely on it?
P: The **Markov property** means "the present is enough". To predict tomorrow's weather from a full weather map, you do not need last month's maps. The current map already holds everything useful.

Q: Explain the chain Markov process, MRP, MDP. What does each add?
P: Build it up like layers. A **Markov chain** is a board game where dice move you around, with no choices and no points. A **Markov reward process** adds points for landing on squares. A **Markov decision process** adds choices, so you pick your move and the dice still add some randomness.

@card w3
I: discount
Q: What is the return and why do we discount?
P: The **return** is the total score from now until the end. **Discounting** means rewards later count a bit less, like money: 100 dollars today is worth more than 100 dollars in ten years.
It also keeps the total finite when the game never ends, and it reflects that the far future is less certain. A discount close to 1 means a patient agent, close to 0 means a short-sighted one.
A: Return $G_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t+2} + \gamma^2 R_{t+3} + \dots = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^k R_{t+k+1}$.
Reasons to discount with $\gamma < 1$:
- Keeps the sum finite in continuing tasks: $|G_t| \le R_{max}/(1-\gamma)$.
- Models uncertainty about the future and the preference for reward sooner.
- Mathematically convenient (Bellman operators become contractions).
Note: $\gamma$ is the discount factor, not "future rewards". $\gamma = 0$ is myopic, only the immediate reward counts. $\gamma = 1$ is fine in episodic tasks that always terminate.
K: return, discount factor, episodic, continuing

Q: Define the state-value and action-value functions and explain the intuitive difference.
P: The **state value** $v$ answers "how good is it to be here?" Like how good a chess position is overall. The **action value** $q$ answers "how good is it to make this specific move from here?"
$v$ rates the place, $q$ rates each option at that place. If you know $q$, picking the best move is easy: choose the highest one.
A: $v_\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi[G_t \mid S_t = s]$: how good it is to **be** in $s$ if you then follow $\pi$.
$q_\pi(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_\pi[G_t \mid S_t = s, A_t = a]$: how good it is to **take action** $a$ in $s$, then follow $\pi$.
$q$ is more granular because it ranks actions. That is exactly what you need to improve a policy without a model, since $\pi'(s) = \arg\max_a q(s,a)$ needs no transition probabilities.
Note: values are **expected discounted returns**, not single rewards.
K: state-value function, action-value function, expected return

@card w4
I: backup
Q: State the Bellman expectation equation and explain each symbol.
P: The **Bellman equation** says: the value of where you are now equals the reward you get next plus the discounted value of where you land, averaged over what might happen.
Like valuing a house as next year's rent plus what the house will be worth next year. You break a long future into "one step, then the rest".
A: $$v_\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a\mid s) \sum_{s',r} p(s',r\mid s,a)\,[\,r + \gamma v_\pi(s')\,]$$
- $\pi(a\mid s)$: probability the policy picks $a$.
- $p(s',r\mid s,a)$: probability the environment moves to $s'$ with reward $r$.
- $r + \gamma v_\pi(s')$: immediate reward plus discounted value of where you land.
In words: value of a state equals the average, over what you might do and what might happen, of immediate reward plus discounted next value. It is **linear** in $v_\pi$, so it has a unique solution for a fixed policy.
Taxi example with $\gamma=1$ and -1 per step: if you are 8 steps from home, $v = -8$, and the next state has $v=-7$, so $-8 = -1 + (-7)$.
K: Bellman expectation equation, policy, transition probability, recursion

Q: How are v and q related? Write both one-step lookaheads.
P: $v$ and $q$ point at each other. The value of a place is the average of the values of the moves your policy would make there. The value of a move is the reward it gives plus the value of the place it takes you.
Like a restaurant's rating being the average of its dishes, and each dish's rating depending on how you feel afterwards.
A: State to action: $v_\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a\mid s)\, q_\pi(s,a)$. Average the action values using the policy.
Action to state: $q_\pi(s,a) = R(s,a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s'\mid s,a)\, v_\pi(s')$. Immediate reward plus discounted expected next value.
Substitute one into the other to get each equation in terms of itself. The optimal versions replace the policy average with a max: $v_*(s) = \max_a q_*(s,a)$.

@card w4
I: backup
Q: What is the Bellman optimality equation and why can't you solve it with a matrix inverse?
P: The **optimality** version swaps "average over my usual moves" for "take the best move". It describes perfect play.
That "take the best" (the max) makes the equation non-linear, like a fork in a road where you always pick one branch. A matrix inverse only solves straight-line (linear) equations, so instead we solve it step by step, iterating until it settles.
A: $$v_*(s) = \max_a \sum_{s',r} p(s',r\mid s,a)[r + \gamma v_*(s')]$$
$$q_*(s,a) = \sum_{s',r} p(s',r\mid s,a)[r + \gamma \max_{a'} q_*(s',a')]$$
The **max** makes it **non-linear**, so there is no closed-form $(I-\gamma P)^{-1}$ solution. You solve it iteratively: value iteration, policy iteration, Q-learning, SARSA.
Once you have $q_*$, the optimal policy is just $\pi_*(s) = \arg\max_a q_*(s,a)$.
K: Bellman optimality equation, non-linear, max operator, iterative methods

Q: What is an optimal policy? Is it unique? Does a deterministic one always exist?
P: An **optimal policy** is a strategy that does at least as well as any other strategy from every starting point.
There can be several, like two equally fast routes to work, but they all share the same best values. And there is always at least one that never needs to flip a coin: in every situation it just picks one best move.
A: $\pi_*$ is optimal if $v_{\pi_*}(s) \ge v_\pi(s)$ for **every** state and every policy $\pi$.
- For any finite MDP an optimal policy exists, and all optimal policies share the same $v_*$ and $q_*$.
- It need not be unique: ties between equally good actions give several optimal policies.
- A **deterministic** optimal policy always exists for an MDP: act greedily on $q_*$.
Exception: in partially observable settings or games, the best policy may need to be stochastic.
K: optimal policy, deterministic policy, existence, uniqueness

Q: How do you solve the Bellman equation for an MRP directly, and when is that a bad idea?

@card w5
I: backup
Q: What is dynamic programming in RL, and what two properties make it work?
P: **Dynamic programming** is solving a big problem by reusing answers to smaller ones, like working out the fastest route home by first knowing the fastest route from each neighbouring street.
It needs two things. The problem must split into smaller copies of itself (**optimal substructure**), and the small answers must get reused many times (**overlapping subproblems**). The Bellman equation gives both. And DP needs the full rules of the game.
A: **DP** computes optimal policies **given a perfect model** of the MDP, by turning the Bellman equations into update rules.
- **Optimal substructure**: the optimal value of a state is built from optimal values of its successors (Bellman equation).
- **Overlapping sub-problems**: the same states are reached by many paths, so their values are computed once and reused. The value function is the cache.
Limitation: needs the full model and full sweeps over the state space, so it suffers from the curse of dimensionality.

Q: Walk through iterative policy evaluation, including the stopping rule.
P: **Iterative policy evaluation** is like gossip spreading through a town. Start with every state's value at zero. Each round, every state updates its value from its neighbours' values plus the reward. After many rounds the values settle.
You stop when a full round changes nothing by more than a tiny amount, the threshold you chose.
A: 1. Initialise $V(s)$ arbitrarily, terminal states at 0.
2. Sweep over all states: $V(s) \leftarrow \sum_a \pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s',r} p(s',r\mid s,a)[r + \gamma V(s')]$.
3. Track $\Delta = \max_s |v_{old} - V(s)|$.
4. Stop when $\Delta < \theta$.
It converges to $v_\pi$ because the Bellman expectation backup is a contraction for $\gamma<1$ (or with guaranteed termination).
4x4 gridworld (equiprobable random policy, -1 per step): values converge to minus the expected number of steps to the exit, and acting greedily on them is optimal after only 3 sweeps.
K: policy evaluation, sweep, convergence threshold, gridworld

Q: Explain policy improvement and the policy improvement theorem.
P: **Policy improvement** means: once you know how good each move is, switch to the best move in every state. Like a delivery driver who, after a week of timing routes, always takes the fastest street at each corner.
The **policy improvement theorem** guarantees that doing this never makes things worse. You either improve or you are already optimal.
A: Given $v_\pi$, build $\pi'(s) = \arg\max_a q_\pi(s,a)$ (greedy).
**Theorem**: if $q_\pi(s,\pi'(s)) \ge v_\pi(s)$ for all $s$, then $v_{\pi'}(s) \ge v_\pi(s)$ for all $s$. Intuition: taking a better action once and then following $\pi$ is better, so taking it every time is at least as good.
If greedy improvement changes nothing, then $v_\pi$ satisfies the Bellman optimality equation, so $\pi$ is already optimal. That is the stopping condition.
K: policy improvement, greedy, policy improvement theorem

Q: Compare policy iteration and value iteration.
P: **Policy iteration** is "fully grade the plan, then improve it, repeat". Few rounds, but each one is slow.
**Value iteration** is "do one quick grading pass and improve at the same time, repeat". Many cheap rounds. Both reach the same best answer, like taking a few long strides or many short steps to the same place.
A: **Policy iteration**: evaluate the policy fully (many sweeps), then improve greedily. Repeat until the policy is stable. Few outer iterations, each expensive. Converges in a finite number of iterations because there are finitely many deterministic policies and each step strictly improves until optimal.
**Value iteration**: one sweep of the **Bellman optimality** backup $V(s) \leftarrow \max_a \sum p[r + \gamma V(s')]$, repeated until $V$ converges, then extract the greedy policy. Each iteration cheap, many iterations.
Value iteration is policy iteration with evaluation truncated to one sweep. Both are special cases of **GPI** and give the same optimal answer.
K: policy iteration, value iteration, GPI, Bellman optimality

Q: What is generalised policy iteration (GPI) and why is it the most important idea in the course?
P: **GPI** is the dance between two partners. One partner (evaluation) asks "how good is my current plan?". The other (improvement) says "then let us change the plan to do better". They keep going until neither has anything to change.
Almost every RL method in the course is this same dance with different steps: DP, Monte Carlo, TD, SARSA, Q-learning and actor-critic.
A: **GPI** is the pattern of two interacting processes: **evaluation** (make the value function match the policy) and **improvement** (make the policy greedy with respect to the value function). They compete locally but converge together to $v_*$ and $\pi_*$, since the only stable point is where the policy is greedy on its own value, which is the Bellman optimality equation.
Nearly every method is GPI with a different evaluation step: DP (full model backups), MC control (sample returns), SARSA and Q-learning (TD), actor-critic (critic evaluates, actor improves).
K: GPI, evaluation, improvement, unifying framework

Q: Synchronous vs asynchronous DP: what is the difference, and why use asynchronous?
P: Synchronous is like a class where everyone hands in their test, the teacher marks them all, and only then are the grades updated. Asynchronous is like the teacher updating each grade the moment one paper is marked, so the next student's result can already use it.
A: **Synchronous DP.** All states are updated at the same time, using the values from the previous iteration.
$$V_{k+1}(s) = R(s) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s'\mid s)\,V_k(s')$$
You compute the new value of every state, store them separately, then replace the old table once all updates are finished. Pro: easy to understand and analyse.
**Asynchronous DP.** States are updated one at a time, and new values can be used immediately.
1. Update state A.
2. Use the new value of A when updating B.
3. Use the new value of B when updating C.
You do not have to wait for a full sweep through all states. Pros: often converges faster in practice, and can focus computation on important states, for example the ones the agent actually visits.
**Simple comparison** (synchronous vs asynchronous)
- **Updates:** all states together vs one state at a time.
- **Values used:** old values throughout the sweep vs newly updated values immediately.
- **Trade-off:** simpler vs often faster.
- **Coverage:** full sweep required vs can update selected states.
Exam line: asynchronous DP still converges as long as every state keeps getting updated.
K: asynchronous DP, in-place updates, prioritised sweeping

Q: Is DP efficient? What is its real limitation?
P: DP is efficient compared with blindly trying every possible strategy, which would take forever. The real problem is that the number of states explodes. Add one more thing to track and the table can multiply by ten.
It also needs the full rules of the world, which you rarely have in real life.
A: In a formal sense yes. DP is **polynomial** in the number of states and actions, while searching every deterministic policy costs $|A|^{|S|}$. It also scales better than linear programming.
The real problem is the **curse of dimensionality**: the number of states grows exponentially with the number of state variables, plus DP needs a **perfect model**. MC and TD remove the model requirement, function approximation tackles the size.
K: polynomial time, curse of dimensionality, perfect model

Q: Briefly describe Jack's Car Rental and the Gambler's Problem. What do they show?
P: **Jack's Car Rental** is a business owner deciding how many cars to move between two shops overnight so that neither runs out. **Gambler's Problem** is deciding how much to bet on each coin flip to reach 100 dollars.
Both show that DP finds strategies a human would never guess, like "bet everything at 50 but only 1 dollar at 51", as long as you know the rules exactly.
A: **Jack's Car Rental** (policy iteration): two locations, Poisson rentals and returns, move up to 5 cars overnight at 2 per car, earn 10 per rental. Policy iteration goes from "never move cars" to the optimal policy in about four improvements.
**Gambler's Problem** (value iteration): capital 1 to 99, bet on coin flips with heads probability 0.4, reward +1 only on reaching 100. The optimal betting policy is jagged and non-obvious (bet everything at 50, not at 51).
They show DP finds optimal policies that intuition would miss, provided you know the model.
K: policy iteration, value iteration, worked examples
